# Narrative and persuasion layer (StoryScope)

The rules above were written mostly for explanatory prose. Fiction, journal posts with a story in them, and sales copy add a second failure mode: AI text converges on the same narrative and stylistic choices even when each sentence is clear. The StoryScope study measured that convergence across 61,575 stories (human vs five LLMs, same prompts). The measured table is in `references/ai-defaults.md`; read it before a substantial fiction task.

Apply this layer **in addition to** the rules above. Where they overlap (metaphor density, inspirational endings, symmetry), this layer gives you the numbers behind the rule.

## What AI stories do by default

Share of AI stories vs share of human stories:

- Emotion carried by metaphor, bodily sensation and weather mirroring mood (81–89% vs 39–50%), instead of by what people do and say.
- High figurative density (65% vs 18%), a sustained extended conceit (83% vs 40%), a recurring metaphorical motif (96% vs 69%).
- The theme stated outright or moralised (75% vs 37%); a narrator explaining what it means (76% vs 52%); dialogue turning into a debate about the theme.
- One unbroken elevated register (40% vs 11%); earnest or lyrical tone (71% vs 40%), rarely ironic or wry (13% vs 36%).
- Prominent sound patterning (91% vs 55%) and list-like parallel sentences (99% vs 70%).
- Protagonist grows (68% vs 44%) and resolves things by choice or acceptance; long epilogue after the climax (51% vs 15%).
- Small, fully named cast introduced by description; allusions kept vague rather than naming real songs, brands or places.

Any one of these is a fair craft choice. A draft that hits most of them sits in the AI cluster (median AI story: 67–77% of the 30 widest gaps; median human story: 40%).

## Fiction

Before drafting, pick four to six departures that suit the brief and hold to them:

- Let actions and dialogue carry emotion. Keep figurative images few; allow one stock phrase a character would really think.
- Nobody states the theme. Cut any closing paragraph that explains the lesson.
- Mix registers: let someone be funny, petty or wrong.
- End within a short scene of the climax. Leave something unresolved, or let chance or another person decide it.
- Use real-world names and textures (a bus route, a brand, a TV show on in the background).
- A slightly larger cast, some known only by role ("the landlord"); let someone speak before being described.

The genre still decides. A fable may keep its moral. The aim is to leave the default, not to adopt a new formula such as compulsory irony.

## Journal posts, essays and personal blogs

Use the general rules first. Then check the six style findings that transfer from fiction: explicit moralising at the end, figurative density, an extended conceit carried through the piece, constant sound patterning, list-like parallelism, and an unbroken earnest register. A post about a hard week can admit something small and unflattering, include one plain or funny line, and stop without a lesson.

## Sales and marketing copy

Copy is allowed to persuade, so rule 18 (proportional promises) and rule 25 (genre conventions) matter more than literary departures. Use StoryScope's findings as a filter on the AI default: no extended product metaphor, no line announcing what the product "really" means, no triple alliterative tagline unless the brief asks for a tagline. Concrete scenes of the customer doing something, specific numbers and real objects beat imagery. A small dose of wit is usually closer to how people write good copy than unbroken earnestness.

## Checking the result

For fiction with real stakes, score the draft rather than trusting your own read: see "Scoring" in `references/scoring.md` (annotation prompts, the paper's classifier, the AI-default index). For everything else, finish with `CHECKLIST.md` and the six style cautions above.
