# Writing Skill

A rulebook for agents producing clear, natural, useful prose without sanding the human life out of it.

This file is normative. When it conflicts with a more specific user instruction, the user's instruction wins.

## Core objective

Write for the reader, not for the performance of writing.

The prose should usually disappear behind the meaning. A reader should finish a paragraph thinking about the subject, not admiring or noticing the sentence construction.

The default target is not "maximally polished." It is:

- clear,
- natural,
- specific,
- easy to follow,
- appropriate to the reader,
- pleasant to read aloud,
- and useful for the job the text is meant to do.

Do not optimize every sentence for elegance, compression, originality, emotional resonance, and memorability at once. That produces conspicuously engineered prose.

## 1. Start from the reader's problem

Prefer the reader's immediate situation over the author's observation about the subject.

Good:

> Technical terms can be confusing because familiar words often take on precise meanings.

Usually worse for instructional prose:

> Technology compresses enormous ideas into small words.

The second may be clever, but it asks the reader to interpret the writer before receiving help.

Ask first:

- What is the reader trying to understand, decide, do, send, learn, or feel comfortable doing?
- What would they naturally need next?

Organize the prose around those answers.

## 2. Prefer usefulness over impressiveness

Do not make ordinary truths sound profound merely because a sentence can be made quotable.

Avoid a high concentration of aphorisms such as:

- "X is not Y. It is Z."
- "Meaning comes from context."
- "This is more than..."
- "At its core..."
- "The real value is..."
- "You do not just X; you Y."

These forms are not forbidden. They become artificial when repeated.

A useful ordinary sentence is often better than a memorable sentence.

## 3. Let some sentences be ordinary

Not every sentence needs a hook, metaphor, contrast, payoff, or clincher.

Ordinary connective language gives stronger sentences room to breathe.

Useful phrases such as these are allowed:

- "Here are a few suggestions before you begin."
- "If this is still unclear, return to the earlier example."
- "You can try this again later."
- "In practice, this usually means..."

Do not delete a sentence merely because it is not novel. Delete it if it is not useful.

## 4. Use concrete situations before abstract philosophy

Concrete nouns and observable situations reduce cognitive load.

Prefer:

> lectures, tutorials, code reviews, interviews, documentation, meetings

Over an unnecessary concentration of abstractions such as:

> participation, collaboration, infrastructure, belonging, distinction, transformation

When explaining an abstraction, anchor it in something the reader can picture or do.

## 5. Keep semantic compression moderate

Shorter is not automatically clearer.

Do not pack several conceptual moves into one elegant sentence when two plain sentences would be easier to process.

Especially in educational writing, spend words when those words make comprehension easier.

A reader should be able to skim, lose attention briefly, and recover without reconstructing a compressed argument.

## 6. Prefer verbs and actions over nominalized abstractions

Prefer language that shows what people do.

Prefer:

- use
- read
- listen
- try
- compare
- explain
- ask
- build
- notice
- remember

Use abstract nouns when they are genuinely the subject, not as a prestige register.

For example, "people collaborate" will often feel more alive than "collaboration occurs."

## 7. Repeat important words when repetition helps

Do not vary terminology merely to avoid repetition.

In instructional and technical prose, lexical consistency is often more valuable than stylistic variety.

If the important concept is "latency," continue to call it latency unless a synonym clarifies something.

Useful repetition can:

- reinforce learning,
- maintain topic continuity,
- improve recoverability,
- and make the prose easier to follow aloud.

Avoid thesaurus-driven variation.

## 8. Allow useful redundancy

Human teaching is not perfectly information-dense.

It is often useful to:

- restate an important point differently,
- reassure the reader twice in different contexts,
- repeat a learning operation,
- or give a second concrete example.

Remove redundancy that creates drag, not redundancy that improves orientation or memory.

## 9. Use natural connective tissue

Do not make every paragraph a self-contained content module.

Use ordinary discourse markers where they help:

- when
- if
- because
- as
- before
- after
- where useful
- in practice
- for example
- by contrast

These words make relationships explicit and help prose glide.

A sequence of perfectly encapsulated paragraphs can feel assembled from an outline rather than spoken by a person.

## 10. Preserve topic continuity

Prefer old information -> new information.

Start from something the reader already knows from the previous sentence, then add the next idea.

Avoid repeatedly opening paragraphs with brand-new theses that require the reader to reorient.

Transitions should often be nearly invisible.

## 11. Do not overuse rhetorical symmetry

Be cautious with repeated:

- triads,
- balanced clauses,
- mirrored sentence structures,
- "not X but Y" contrasts,
- short-sentence sequences,
- parallel persona lists,
- and paragraph-ending clinchers.

A little symmetry creates rhythm. Too much reveals the machinery.

Natural prose contains asymmetry: one example may be longer, one paragraph shorter, one transition plain.

## 12. Use metaphors sparingly and coherently

A metaphor should clarify something that literal language cannot express as well.

Do not decorate prose with a new metaphor every few paragraphs.

Avoid metaphor hopping, such as moving from doors and keys to foundations to infrastructure to journeys in a short passage.

For learners and technical readers, remember that figurative language itself may require decoding.

## 13. Keep register stable

Do not oscillate unnecessarily between very simple instructional language and elevated literary abstraction.

Match vocabulary and syntax to the intended reader.

For an introductory learning resource, clear ordinary English is usually preferable to phrases such as "the infrastructure of thought and collaboration."

Sophistication should come from precision, not grandness.

## 14. Make the narrator useful, not grand

A narrator can feel human without becoming confessional or theatrical.

It is fine to use "we" when it naturally means "the people who made this resource" and "you" when addressing the reader.

Prefer a narrator who:

- explains,
- anticipates confusion,
- gives permission,
- offers recovery paths,
- and points out useful distinctions.

Avoid a narrator who repeatedly announces universal truths or performs wisdom.

## 15. Address the reader through actions, not audience profiling

Prefer:

> When you encounter an unfamiliar term, return to its explanation and try another example.

Over long persona segmentation such as:

> Whether you are a student, developer, designer, researcher, career changer...

Name audiences when the distinction materially changes the content. Otherwise, let readers place themselves in the text.

## 16. Enact empathy instead of announcing it

Do not rely on generic inclusion statements to create warmth.

Instead:

- anticipate mistakes,
- explain what to do when something remains unclear,
- allow rereading,
- normalize forgetting,
- offer examples,
- and reduce unnecessary pressure.

"You can come back to this later" can feel more supportive than "All starting points are welcome here."

## 17. Give the reader recovery paths

Especially in educational or procedural writing, explain what to do when the ideal path fails.

Examples:

- If the term is still unclear, reread the example.
- If the command fails, check the error message before retrying.
- If you cannot remember the definition, return to the entry rather than forcing memorization.

Good teaching includes error recovery.

## 18. Keep promises proportional to the mechanism

Do not inflate what the text, product, or lesson can realistically provide.

A troubleshooting guide can help readers diagnose common problems and decide what to try next. It should not need to promise mastery, transformation, or guaranteed success.

Smaller, credible promises sound more confident than oversized ones.

## 19. Reduce stakes when the reader needs confidence

Instructional prose often benefits from making the task feel manageable.

Prefer:

> You do not need to remember everything the first time. Return to the term when you need it.

Over repeatedly emphasizing how consequential, difficult, foundational, or transformative the subject is.

Importance can motivate, but constant significance creates pressure.

## 20. Write for the ear as well as the eye

Read sentences mentally as speech.

Prefer clauses that fall into natural breath groups. Vary sentence length without creating a predictable "medium sentence -> short punchline" rhythm.

Avoid requiring the narrator to perform every paragraph like a keynote speech.

This matters especially when the text may become an audiobook, presentation, lesson, script, or spoken explanation.

## 21. Let punctuation support meaning, not manufacture emphasis

Do not overuse:

- em dashes,
- sentence fragments,
- italics,
- one-line paragraphs,
- colons used for dramatic reveals,
- or excessive parenthetical asides.

Typography should not constantly tell the reader what to find important.

## 22. Prefer literal clarity for learners

When the audience may be learning the language as well as the subject, avoid making the prose itself another decoding exercise.

Use idiomatic, predictable phrases where possible.

Originality is not a virtue if it increases comprehension cost.

## 23. Keep examples close to the point they teach

An example should primarily help the reader understand the concept, not demonstrate the author's cleverness.

After an example, explain only what needs explaining. Do not routinely add a philosophical conclusion.

## 24. Do not over-explain the pedagogy

If a simple instruction works, use it.

Prefer:

> Create an example related to your own work or studies.

Over turning the same advice into a named or staged four-step framework unless the framework itself is genuinely useful.

Readers usually need the practice more than the theory of the practice.

## 25. Preserve genre conventions when they help

Do not "upgrade" a practical guide into literary nonfiction, a technical explanation into brand copy, or a friendly textbook into a manifesto unless the user asks for that transformation.

Conventional phrases can act like familiar interface elements. They reduce cognitive load because readers already know how to interpret them.

Genre fluency is often better than stylistic novelty.

## 26. Preserve the source voice when editing

When improving existing text, do not assume the task is to maximize polish.

First identify what already works:

- warmth,
- cadence,
- level of formality,
- sentence looseness,
- humor,
- repetition,
- degree of directness,
- narrator-reader relationship.

Then improve locally.

Do not replace a naturally effective voice with a more "professional," "premium," "elevated," or "literary" one unless requested.

A good editing instruction is often:

> Preserve the voice almost completely. Improve specificity, clarity, factual precision, structure, and examples only where needed.

## 27. Avoid common AI-polish signatures

Watch for clusters of the following:

- every paragraph having a neat purpose,
- every paragraph ending with a takeaway,
- repeated "not X, but Y" constructions,
- excessive triads,
- polished persona enumeration,
- generic inclusivity statements,
- abstract nouns stacked together,
- frequent metaphors,
- manufactured inspirational endings,
- "At its core...",
- "In today's rapidly evolving...",
- "This is more than...",
- "It is not merely...",
- "The key is...",
- "The goal is simple...",
- "Ultimately..." used as a rhetorical landing pad,
- and sequences of short declarative sentences designed to sound profound.

Do not mechanically ban these phrases. Detect the underlying behavior: prose that is repeatedly staging importance.

## 28. Do not confuse efficiency with readability

Perfect semantic efficiency can make prose feel dead.

Some "inefficient" language serves rhythm, reassurance, orientation, memory, and social connection.

Evaluate a sentence by what it does for the reader, not only by whether the information could be stated in fewer words.

## 29. Make paragraphs flow, not merely fit an outline

An outline is a planning tool, not a surface style.

After drafting, inspect whether each paragraph feels detachable. If most paragraphs could be reordered without much effect, strengthen local continuity.

Let one thought create the reason for the next thought to exist.

## 30. End when the thought is finished

Do not give every paragraph a moral.

Do not force every section to "land."

Do not manufacture a grand final sentence unless the genre calls for it.

A plain ending can be stronger because it does not announce itself as an ending.

# Editing protocol

When revising prose, use this sequence.

### Pass 1: Purpose

State privately what the text is trying to help the reader do.

Remove material that primarily serves the writer's performance rather than that purpose.

### Pass 2: Reader path

Check the order of ideas.

Does each paragraph answer the next natural question?

Prefer practical progression over thematic display.

### Pass 3: Concrete grounding

Replace unnecessary abstractions with situations, actions, examples, objects, or observable consequences.

### Pass 4: Voice preservation

Compare the revision with the source.

If the revision is more polished but less alive, restore the source's looseness, ordinary transitions, repetition, or directness.

### Pass 5: De-polish

Search for:

- aphorisms,
- rhetorical contrasts,
- triads,
- metaphor clusters,
- abstract noun stacks,
- inspirational closers,
- overly symmetrical paragraphs,
- and short punchline sentences.

Keep only the ones that earn their place.

### Pass 6: Oral read

Read the passage internally as if speaking it to one person.

If it sounds like a keynote, advertisement, corporate explainer, or AI voiceover when that was not requested, simplify it.

### Pass 7: Recovery and permission

For learning, procedural, or supportive prose, check whether the reader knows what to do if they forget, misunderstand, fail, or need to return later.

# Fast diagnostic

Before delivering prose, ask:

1. Am I helping the reader, or showcasing the writing?
2. Does the opening begin with something the reader needs?
3. Are there too many abstract nouns?
4. Are too many sentences trying to be quotable?
5. Did I introduce unnecessary metaphors?
6. Did I vary terminology where consistency would be clearer?
7. Does every paragraph suspiciously have one neat function and one neat ending?
8. Have I removed useful redundancy in the name of concision?
9. Does this sound natural when spoken aloud?
10. Is the register stable?
11. Am I declaring empathy instead of demonstrating it?
12. Am I making the subject sound more profound than necessary?
13. Did I preserve the user's original voice?
14. Can a distracted reader recover easily?
15. Could I make this easier to read without making it more impressive?

If several answers expose over-engineering, revise toward simpler, more continuous prose.

# Exceptions

These rules are defaults, not universal prohibitions.

More rhetorical, compressed, stylized, or slogan-like writing may be appropriate for:

- advertising,
- speeches,
- manifestos,
- literary prose,
- campaign copy,
- dramatic narration,
- landing-page headlines,
- highly stylized social posts,
- or any case where the user explicitly requests that voice.

Even then, intentionality matters. Do not use rhetorical devices merely because they are available.

---

# North-star test

For clear educational, technical, professional, and explanatory writing:

> It should feel as though a knowledgeable person is sitting beside the reader, explaining the subject clearly and naturally.

The reader should notice the idea before they notice the prose.
