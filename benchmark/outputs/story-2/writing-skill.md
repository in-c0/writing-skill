# Unit 114

The lease on unit 114 ran out at five o'clock on Sunday. Denise had already extended it once, in October, and she had decided she would not do it again. She told the manager that on Friday when she picked up the gate code, and he said that was fine, people did it all the time, and wrote the code on the back of a receipt.

Lorrie got there at nine-forty on Saturday with two coffees in a cardboard tray. Denise had been there since eight. She had pulled the door up, swept a path down the middle of the concrete, and put strips of colored tape on the first dozen boxes: green to keep, yellow for the church thrift store, red for the dumpster she'd paid forty dollars to use.

"I didn't know if you still take it with cream," Lorrie said, holding out a cup.

"Black."

"Oh. Sorry. I'll drink both." She looked past Denise into the unit. "God. He kept all of it."

He had. Their father had moved out of the house on Alder Street four years earlier, into a one-bedroom apartment with a balcony that looked at a parking lot, and everything that didn't fit in the apartment had come here. Denise had arranged the move. She had also arranged the apartment, the hearing aids, the visiting nurse, and, in March, the funeral, which Lorrie had flown in for on the morning of and flown out of the next day.

They worked without saying much. It was easier than Denise expected, mostly because there was so much to decide and none of it mattered. A rowing machine with a cracked seat went red. Three boxes of Reader's Digest condensed books went yellow. Lorrie wanted to keep a carton of coffee cans full of screws, each can labeled by size in their father's square capitals.

"You live in an apartment in Tucson," Denise said.

"I could use screws."

"You could buy screws."

Lorrie put the cans down. "Fine. Red."

"Yellow. Somebody will want them."

Around noon they found the bunk bed. It was at the back, behind the sewing machine that had been their mother's, and it had been taken apart with care. Every rail was wrapped in a moving blanket. The bolts were in sandwich bags taped to the pieces they belonged to, and on each bag their father had written where it went: TOP LEFT HEAD, LADDER LOWER, and so on.

Lorrie sat down on the concrete. "We fought over the top bunk for like six years."

"You got it."

"I got it because you went to college."

"I got the whole room when I went to college. You got the top bunk in an empty room."

Lorrie laughed, which Denise hadn't heard in a long time, and then stopped. "Why would he keep this?"

Denise didn't know. She had been in this unit maybe five times and had never looked behind the sewing machine. She said so, and Lorrie looked at her for a moment as if deciding whether to believe it.

They ate sandwiches sitting on the tailgate of Denise's car. A man two units down was loading a jet ski onto a trailer, and they both watched him do it because it was something to watch.

"You could have told me how bad it was," Lorrie said. "Last winter."

Denise had been waiting for this since about Thursday. She'd rehearsed a few answers in the car. What she actually said was, "You could have called."

"I called."

"You called on his birthday. You called at Christmas. He'd tell you he was fine and you'd believe him because it was easier."

"He told me he was fine."

"He told everyone he was fine. He told the nurse he was fine while she was changing his catheter."

Lorrie didn't say anything. The man with the jet ski got it strapped down and drove off, and the gate rolled shut behind him.

"I'm not saying it to be mean," Denise said, though she wasn't sure that was true.

"I know you're not," Lorrie said, which was generous of her, and they went back to work.

By six they had done about two-thirds. Denise drove the first load to the thrift store, which had closed at five, so she left everything in the covered drop-off area and felt guilty about it the whole way home. Lorrie was staying at the Hampton Inn by the highway. Denise had thought about offering her the guest room and then hadn't, and Lorrie hadn't asked.

On Sunday Lorrie was there first. She'd brought coffee again, and one of them was black.

The last third was paper. There were tax returns going back to 1981, which Denise had looked up and could shred. There were manuals for appliances they no longer owned, and a folder of warranties for the same appliances. There was a box with LORRIE written on the side in marker.

Lorrie opened it standing up. Inside were report cards, a ribbon from a science fair, the program from her eighth-grade band concert with her name circled, and a stack of postcards. Denise recognized them after a second. They were the ones Lorrie had sent the summer she drove across the country after college, one from nearly every state, most of them saying almost nothing. Hot here. Saw a bison. Car is making the noise again.

"I didn't think he read these," Lorrie said.

"He kept them on the fridge for about a year. Mom made him take them down."

"I didn't know that."

"You weren't here."

She hadn't meant it as a jab that time, only as a fact, and Lorrie seemed to understand. She sat down on a box of tax returns and read through the postcards one at a time. Denise went on with the warranties.

There wasn't a box with Denise's name on it. She looked, because she couldn't help it, and then stopped, because she thought she knew why. She had never left. He'd had no reason to save anything of hers when he could pick up the phone and she would be over in twenty minutes.

At three-thirty the unit was empty except for the bunk bed. They stood looking at it.

"I can't take it on a plane," Lorrie said.

"I know."

"Do you have room?"

Denise had a garage with one car in it and a lot of space where her ex-husband's workbench used to be. "For now," she said.

They carried it out piece by piece, and the bags of bolts stayed taped where their father had put them. When the car was full, Denise pulled the door down and they walked the key to the office. The manager asked if they wanted to renew for another month. Denise said no, and he crossed them off a list.

In the parking lot Lorrie held on to the box with her name on it. Her flight was at seven.

"I could come back in the spring," she said. "We could put it together. See if it still works."

"It's a bed, Lorrie. It works."

"You know what I mean."

Denise did. She said the spring was fine, and that she'd leave the bolts where they were.
