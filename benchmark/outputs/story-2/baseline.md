# Unit 114

The lease on Unit 114 ran out at five o'clock Sunday. Joanne had the paperwork in a plastic sleeve on the passenger seat, as if someone might stop her at the gate and ask for proof that she was allowed to be sad here.

Tess was already there, sitting on the bumper of a rented van with two coffees. She held one out before Joanne had both feet on the asphalt.

"I didn't know how you take it now," Tess said.

"Same as before."

"That's what I guessed." She didn't say she'd guessed right. Neither of them checked.

The roller door went up with a sound like a long breath through teeth. Their father had been dead eleven weeks, and the unit still smelled of him: machine oil, cedar, the cough drops he kept in every pocket. Steel shelving ran floor to ceiling on three sides. On every shelf sat something broken.

A toaster. Four lamps. A child's record player shaped like a suitcase. A sewing machine with its needle bar bent sideways. A box fan, a typewriter, an entire vacuum cleaner in a black bin bag. Each one wore a manila tag tied on with garden twine, and on each tag, in his square capitals, a name and a fault.

*MRS. ODUYA — ELEMENT.*
*KEV (NO. 9) — WON'T HOLD TUNE.*
*CHURCH — CORD FRAYED, DO NOT PLUG IN.*

"Jesus," Tess said. "I thought it would be boxes. Tax returns. Christmas decorations."

"Those are at the back." Joanne set her coffee on the floor. "This was his retirement."

"Fixing the neighbourhood's toasters."

"Fixing anything anyone brought him." She heard how it sounded, the edge in it, the *you'd know if you'd been here*. She let it stand. Tess had flown in Friday night and would fly out Monday morning. There wasn't time to sand anything down.

They made rules, because rules were easier than talking. Anything with a name went in the van, to be returned. Anything without a name went to the tip. Paper went in a box for Joanne to deal with later, because Joanne dealt with things later; that had been the arrangement for twenty years.

By noon on Saturday they had cleared one wall. Tess worked fast and without ceremony, reading tags aloud like a roll call. Joanne kept stopping. She knew half these people. She had driven their father to Mrs. Oduya's with the repaired kettle the week before his first fall. She had sat in the car while he went in, and he'd come out forty minutes later holding a foil tray of jollof rice, embarrassed and pleased, and said *she insisted* the way other men said *I won*.

"You're doing it again," Tess said.

"Doing what?"

"Holding things." Tess nodded at the typewriter in Joanne's arms. "You've been holding that for a while."

Joanne put it in the van.

They ate sandwiches sitting on upturned crates. A man two units down was moving a jet ski with enormous care. Tess watched him and said, "Remember the boat motor?"

"He never had a boat."

"He had a motor, though. In the garage. For years."

"It's here," Joanne said. "Back left corner. Tagged."

Tess laughed, then stopped laughing, and went to look. The tag said *MINE — ONE DAY*. She stood in front of it for a long time with her back to Joanne, and Joanne didn't say *you're doing it again*, though she wanted to, though it would have been fair.

---

Sunday was the back of the unit, where the shelving gave way to a jumble of furniture under sheets. Their mother's sewing chair. The kitchen table with the burn mark from the pressure cooker. Four dining chairs, one with a wobble their father had been promising to fix since 1994.

And a bicycle. Purple, with streamers gone pale and brittle at the handgrips. A tag was tied around the crossbar.

*T. — CHAIN. ASK.*

Tess went still. Joanne watched her read it twice.

"That's mine," Tess said, which was obvious.

"Yes."

"Why does it say *ask*?"

Joanne had known about the bike for years. She had seen it in the garage, then in the unit, and she had never once thought about telling Tess. Now she understood that this had been a choice, and that she'd made it every time.

"You were fourteen," Joanne said. "The chain kept slipping. He offered to fix it and you told him you'd do it yourself. You said you didn't need him to do everything for you."

"I don't remember that."

"You slammed the door. It was quite a good slam." Joanne picked at the twine. "He said he'd wait until you asked. And then you didn't, and then you were sixteen and you had a car, and then you were in Melbourne, and then—"

"And then I was in Melbourne for nineteen years," Tess said. "You can say it."

"I wasn't going to say it."

"You've been saying it all weekend."

It came out of Joanne before she could stop it. "I drove him to every appointment. I learned how to do his insulin. I sat with him at four in the morning when he thought Mum was in the next room. And he kept *your* bike. For thirty years. Waiting for you to *ask*."

The words hung in the unit with the dust. Down the row, the jet-ski man's roller door clattered shut.

Tess didn't flinch, and didn't cry, which Joanne would have found easier. She crouched by the bike and turned a pedal backwards. The chain caught, slipped, caught.

"He never tagged anything of yours," Tess said quietly. "Did you notice? I've been reading every one. There's nothing with your name on it."

"Because I never brought him anything broken."

"I know." Tess looked up. "That's not the compliment you think it is, Jo. It just means he never got the chance to do it for you either."

Joanne opened her mouth and found nothing in it.

It was four-twenty. The van was full. The tip closed at five, the same time as the lease, as if the whole city had agreed this was when things ended.

Tess stood, wiped her hands on her jeans, and wheeled the bike out into the light. She flipped it upside down on the asphalt so it rested on its seat and handlebars, the way their father used to, and crouched beside it. Then she looked back at Joanne, standing in the empty doorway of Unit 114.

"Can you hold the back wheel," Tess said. "I can't do this one by myself."

It was a small thing. It was nearly nothing. Joanne came out and knelt on the hot ground and held the wheel steady while her sister worked the chain back onto its teeth, link by link, both of them with grease to the wrist, neither of them saying anything at all.

At five the manager came by with his clipboard. The unit was empty. They signed where he told them to sign.
