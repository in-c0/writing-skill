# Unit 217

"You brought gloves?" Dana said. "Of course you brought gloves."

She was standing at the roll-up door of unit 217 with her phone flashlight on, though the overhead bulb worked fine. She had flown in from Phoenix the night before and was wearing white sneakers, which told Lori most of what she needed to know about how Dana expected the weekend to go.

"There's a second pair in the car," Lori said.

"I'm fine."

The kid at the front desk had already explained it twice. The lease ended Monday, the gate code stopped working Sunday at six, and anything left after that went to auction or the dumpster, depending on what he called "the vibe of the contents." He'd said sorry for their loss in the same voice he used for the gate code.

Lori pulled the door up the rest of the way. Their father had packed the unit the way he used to pack the trunk for vacations: floor to ceiling, heavy stuff on the bottom, nothing you could get out without getting everything out. The front row was Folgers cans. She counted eleven before she stopped.

"Screws," Dana said, lifting a lid. "Sorted. Oh my God, they're sorted by length."

"He did that on Sundays."

"I know what he did on Sundays, Lori. I lived there for eighteen years."

They worked until noon without talking about anything that mattered. The NordicTrack went to the curb pile. So did a box of National Geographics, a Mr. Coffee with the carafe still in it, and three folding chairs from St. Bridget's that someone should probably return but nobody was going to. Every box had a label in their father's square capitals. Most said MISC. Some said MISC — GOOD.

"What's the difference?" Dana asked, holding one of each.

"One's good."

Dana laughed, actually laughed, and then looked annoyed that she had. Lori kept her eyes on the box in front of her.

The man in 219 had his door up too. He was rebuilding an outboard motor on a blue tarp and had WEEI going on a paint-spattered radio, two hosts yelling about the Patriots' offensive line. Around one he came over with a box of Dunkin' Munchkins and held it out without saying anything. Dana took two. Lori took one, then another when he kept waiting.

"Your dad was Ray?" he said.

"Yeah."

"He used to sit out here in a lawn chair. Just sit. I figured he had a wife at home he didn't like."

"Our mom died in 2011," Dana said.

"Huh." He seemed to think about that. "Well. He liked the Munchkins." He went back to his motor.

After lunch they reached the boxes from the house. Dana found her own report cards in a Market Basket bag, every one from kindergarten through senior year, with the teachers' comments highlighted in yellow. Lori's were in the same bag, but only through fifth grade. She held up the last one so Dana could see the year.

"He probably just stopped keeping them," Dana said.

"For me."

"Lori. You were the one who was here. You got the actual guy. I got a bag."

That was the closest either of them had come, and they both went back to work quickly, Dana breaking down boxes with a lot more force than the cardboard needed.

The ledger was in a tackle box, under a tray of lures he never used because he never fished. It was a green accounting book from Staples, and the first page said DANA in the same capitals as the boxes. Lori read the first line before she understood what she was looking at.

3/14/2016 — 11,000.00

Under it ran a column of entries. $200. $200. $150. $200. Each one was dated and subtracted, and the balance was carried down the right side in pencil. She turned the pages. The payments got smaller and further apart. The last one was in August 2019, for $75, and the balance under it said $6,325.00. After that there was nothing, just ruled lines all the way to the back cover.

Dana had stopped moving. Lori didn't know how long she'd been watching.

"You told me you paid him back," Lori said.

"I did pay him back."

"It says six thousand three hundred and twenty-five dollars."

"I know what it says." Dana held out her hand, and Lori didn't give her the book. "He told me to stop. When Kevin got laid off. He called me and said stop sending it, I'm not cashing them anyway."

"He cashed these."

"He cashed those. Then he told me to stop."

"He never said that to me."

"Why would he say it to you? It wasn't your money." Dana's voice went up, and then she pulled it back down, the way she used to on the phone with their mother. "Do you want it? Is that what this is? You want me to write you a check for half of six thousand dollars?"

Lori thought about nineteen months of driving him to dialysis in Natick, three mornings a week, sitting in a waiting room where the TV was stuck on HGTV. She thought about the Christmas Dana sent an Edible Arrangement. She didn't want the money. She wanted him to have written something on the last page, one line, anything, FORGIVEN or FORGET IT or even DANA SAYS KEVIN, so she would know which of them he had been angry at, if he had been angry at all.

"No," she said. "I don't want a check."

"Then what?"

Lori didn't have an answer. On the radio in 219, a caller was saying the whole coaching staff should be fired, and the hosts were agreeing with him a lot.

"All this going?"

The junk guy had arrived at four, from 1-800-GOT-JUNK, with a clipboard and a back brace. His shirt said HECTOR. He had walked the curb pile and was now looking into the half-empty unit.

"All of it," Dana said.

"The cans too? People take screws, you know. Habitat takes screws."

"Then give them to Habitat."

Hector started loading. He moved faster than either of them had moved all day, and in ten minutes the NordicTrack and the National Geographics and most of MISC were gone. Lori watched him pick up the tackle box. He shook it, heard the lures rattle, and set it on the lift gate with everything else.

The ledger wasn't in it. Lori had seen Dana slip it into her purse while they were arguing, and Dana had seen her see it, and neither of them had said anything.

"I'm keeping one of these," Lori said, and took a Folgers can off the stack before Hector could get to it. She didn't check which one. It was heavier than she expected, and something shifted inside when she tucked it under her arm.

"Number eights," Dana said, reading the masking tape on the lid. "Inch and a quarter."

"Okay."

"You don't even own a drill."

"I know."

Hector pulled the strap down over the load and asked which one of them was signing. Dana looked at Lori. Lori held out her free hand for the clipboard, and after a second Dana gave it to her.
