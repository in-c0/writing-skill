# Unit 114

"If you take the drill, I'm taking the esky," Bec said, before I'd even got the roller door all the way up.

I hadn't seen my sister in four years and that was the first thing she said. She was standing in the bay with a bundle of flat-packed Bunnings boxes under one arm and a coffee in the other hand, wearing Dad's Bulldogs cap, which I thought was a bit much.

"I don't want the drill."

"You say that now."

The unit was three metres by four, according to the contract, which she had printed and put in a plastic sleeve. It was packed to about my chin. A path ran down the middle, one person wide, and you could tell Dad had used it, because the boxes on either side were scuffed at hip height.

The man at the front desk had told us five o'clock Sunday. After that the unit went to "the auction process," which he said the way a doctor says "the procedure." He also told us Dad used to come in every Saturday morning and sit in there for an hour with the door half down.

"Never took anything out," he said. "Never put anything in either, the last couple of years. Just sat."

Bec nodded like this was useful information. I wanted to ask what he sat on.

It was a camp chair. We found it at eleven, under a tarp, facing the back wall.

---

Bec's system was keep, dump, Vinnies. Three piles, three bits of masking tape on the concrete outside, written on in Sharpie. I agreed to it so we wouldn't argue about it, and then we argued about everything that went on it.

The wok, for example. He'd bought it the year Mum left and I don't think it ever touched a flame. Bec put it on keep.

"You don't cook."

"I might."

A box of VHS tapes: *The Castle* twice, *Crocodile Dundee*, and one labelled CRICKET 99 DO NOT TAPE OVER in his capitals. I put them on dump. When I came back from carrying a mattress to the skip they were on keep, stacked neater than I'd left them. I didn't say anything. Neither did she. That was more or less how the four years had gone, too.

Around one her phone rang and it was her youngest on FaceTime, a face I knew only from Christmas photos.

"Is that Aunty Kel?"

"Hi, Mila."

"Mum says you live in Perth."

"I do."

"Mum says it's three hours behind, so you're always late for things."

Bec took the phone off me and walked out into the corridor with it. I heard her say "Because it's a *joke*, darling," and then nothing for a while.

The man in Unit 116 had his roller door up too. He was restoring a jet ski with a Cold Chisel album going on a paint-spattered radio and he wandered over in his thongs and footy shorts to see what we had. He offered fifty for the whipper snipper. Bec said eighty without looking up. They settled on sixty, and he threw in a cold Solo from his esky for each of us, which I think made it a good deal for everyone.

"Your old man," he said, cracking his own can. "Saturdays you could hear his radio through the wall. Racing, mostly."

"We heard," Bec said.

"Nice bloke. Never said much."

"No," I said. "He didn't."

---

Dad's mate Terry rang Bec at five to say the fishing rods had been promised to him. Bec said fine, come and get them. When she hung up I said Dad couldn't stand Terry.

"Dad couldn't stand anyone, for about a week each," she said. "Then he'd go fishing with them."

That was true and I hadn't thought of it in years. I laughed, and she looked surprised, and then she looked annoyed that she'd looked surprised, and we went back to work.

We didn't talk about the hospital. Bec had done the six weeks, the parking, the forms, the night they called her at three. I had sent flowers that the ward wasn't allowed to accept because of some infection rule, and I'd found that out from the florist's refund email. She knew all of this. I knew she knew. She let me carry the heavy things without offering to help, which seemed fair.

---

Sunday afternoon we got to the back wall. Behind the camp chair were four archive boxes, and one of them said KELLY on the side.

I knew what this was supposed to be. Letters. Something he couldn't say out loud. I'd seen the films.

It was my school reports. Every one, from kindy to Year 12, in order. My netball bibs. A certificate for Most Improved in swimming, which I had been, because I'd started out drowning. My Year 9 science fair poster about whether music helps tomato plants grow. It didn't. The control group did best. Someone had written *Good effort* on the back in red pen.

"Where's mine?" Bec said.

I looked at the other three boxes. Tax returns. More tax returns. A box of Mum's things he'd apparently never got around to giving back, a hairdryer, two cookbooks, a cardigan.

There was no box that said REBECCA.

She sat down in the camp chair. I stood there holding the poster.

"You can have it," I said, which was a stupid thing to say.

"I don't want your tomatoes, Kel."

"I mean, he probably—"

"Don't." She took the cap off and turned it around in her hands. "Don't tell me what he probably."

So I didn't. I sat on the KELLY box, because there wasn't anywhere else, and we stayed like that for a bit with the door half down, which I realised afterward was exactly how the man at the desk had described it. Out in the corridor the jet ski guy's radio was doing the cricket scores.

---

At twenty to five the man from the front desk knocked on the door frame with a clipboard.

"Just letting you know, no rush," he said. "Someone came in Friday and paid it through to February."

Bec looked at me. I shook my head.

"It wasn't me," she said.

"Older lady," he said. "Paid cash. Didn't leave a name. Said she was a friend of Graham's." He looked at the keep pile, the wok, the tapes. "Anyway. You've got till February."

He left. Bec stood up and looked at the masking tape outside, and the skip, which was full, and the corridor, which was empty.

"Did he have a—" I started.

"I don't know," she said. "I don't know anything about him, apparently."

When we locked up that night she handed me the drill without a word and I took it. I flew back on the Tuesday. It's in my laundry in Perth. I've never used it. I keep meaning to ring her about February.
