# Three Weeks Out

I signed up for the half marathon on a Tuesday night in July, mostly because a friend sent me a video of her crossing a finish line and I was feeling brave after dinner. The confirmation email arrived before I could talk myself out of it. Seventeen weeks, the plan said. Plenty of time.

At that point I couldn't run five kilometres without stopping. My first "training run" was three kilometres around the block with two walking breaks, and I came home red-faced and weirdly proud. I printed the plan, stuck it to the fridge, and bought a yellow highlighter so I could colour in each session as I finished it. That highlighter became the most important object in my house.

The weeks settled into a shape. Easy runs on Tuesdays and Thursdays, something faster on Wednesdays, the long run on Sunday morning before the paths got busy. I learned which streets had shade and which café opened early enough to sell me a banana. There's an older man with a kelpie who walks the same loop I do, and by September we'd graduated from ignoring each other to a nod, then a wave. I don't know his name. I think about him more than I'd admit.

Things I didn't expect: how much I'd come to love the long runs, how boring the easy ones would get, and how a black toenail can make you feel like a real athlete. I bought proper shoes after my old ones gave me blisters the size of coins. I started going to bed at ten on Saturdays. My friends stopped inviting me to things on Saturday nights, which was fair.

Two weeks ago I ran eighteen kilometres. It was the longest I'd ever gone, and the last four were miserable, and when I stopped my watch I sat on a low wall and laughed at nothing for a while. I texted my sister a screenshot of the distance with no caption. She replied with eleven exclamation marks. I was ready. I could feel it.

Then last Thursday, halfway up the hill on my tempo run, something hit the back of my left leg. That's what it felt like, anyway, as if someone had thrown a stone. I actually turned around to look. There was nobody there. I tried to keep going and my calf just said no, so I limped the two kilometres home, and the walk took longer than the whole run had been supposed to.

The physio pressed her thumb into the muscle and I made a noise I'm not proud of. Grade one calf strain. Mild, she said, as if that would help. Three to four weeks before I should run properly again. The race is in three weeks.

I did the maths in the car park, then did it again as if the numbers might change.

I've spent this week being sulky in a way I find embarrassing. I ate cereal for dinner twice. I stopped looking at the fridge because the plan just ends now, a column of bright yellow and then a column of white. I unfollowed two running accounts that kept posting taper tips. I know it's only a strain. I know people come back from much worse. Knowing doesn't do much.

What I'm doing instead: calf raises against the kitchen bench, slow heel drops off the bottom stair, twenty minutes on a stationary bike at the gym that squeaks on every rotation. Ice. Patience, or my best impression of it. The physio says we'll make a call in ten days. I might start and walk parts of it. I might not start at all. I don't know yet, and I'm trying not to decide the ending before I get there.

This morning I hobbled down to the corner for coffee and the man with the kelpie was coming the other way. He looked at my leg, then at me, and raised his eyebrows. I shrugged. He nodded, like he understood completely, and kept walking.

I'm holding on to that for now.
