# Month One

On the first Monday after I quit, I woke up at 6:40 out of habit and lay there waiting for the part of my brain that checks Slack to settle down. It took about a week.

I had been writing backend code for a logistics company. The team was good, the pay was fine, and there was a 9:15 standup that I resented every single day. Apparently I miss it now. The plan was to build small apps. Not a startup, no investors, no pitch deck. Just small, useful things I would own outright and ship often, until a few of them covered rent.

This is what the first month of that actually looked like.

## It was lonelier than I thought

I knew I would be working alone. I didn't know what alone would feel like at 2pm on a Tuesday. At my old job someone always needed something from me, so loneliness never really came up. Now nobody needs anything. My longest conversation most days is with the person who makes my coffee. Last week I explained a CSS bug out loud to my empty kitchen, start to finish, and the kitchen had no notes.

## It was slower, too

I assumed that without meetings I would get twice as much done. I got about the same amount, maybe less. It turns out meetings were also deadlines, and other people's expectations were doing a lot of the work I had been crediting to my own discipline. On my own, a two-day task stretches to fill a week, mostly because nobody is there to tell me it's good enough to ship.

## What I shipped

Two apps.

The first is a Mac menu bar app that tracks how long you've been sitting and nudges you to stand up. It took eleven days. It has 23 downloads, and at least four of those are me, testing on different machines.

The second is a web tool that turns a recipe link into a shopping list sorted by supermarket aisle. It took six days. I posted it on a forum and got 140 visitors in one afternoon, then it settled back to two or three a day. I suspect most of those are bots.

Nobody wrote to tell me they hated either one. Nobody wrote at all. That was harder than I expected, because silence gives you nothing to fix.

The other surprise was how short the good feeling lasted. Shipping felt great for about an hour. Then the real work started, which is telling people the thing exists, and I am bad at it. I spent years believing the hard part was building. Building turned out to be the part I already knew how to do.

## Was it the right call?

I honestly don't know. I have about nine months of savings at my current spending. That sounds like plenty until I set it next to how long it took to get 23 downloads.

But some things were good. I went for a walk at 11 on a weekday morning. I learned more about App Store review in two weeks than in my whole career before this. I fixed a bug at midnight because I wanted to, not because a pager went off. And both apps are mine, small and ignored as they are.

## Month two

Fewer apps, more talking. I'm going to ship one thing and then spend as long telling people about it as I spent building it. I'll work from the library two days a week so I'm at least near other humans. I'll set deadlines and tell a friend about them, so they count.

I'll check back in a month. If the number next to the menu bar app still says 23, I'll write about that too.
