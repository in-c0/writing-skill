# Number Candles

The first time I noticed him, he bought a 4.

It was a little after three on a Wednesday morning, the quiet part of the shift. The emergency department across the road had mostly finished sending people over with their discharge scripts, and the security guard had gone outside to vape. The man put a single number candle on the counter, the blue and silver kind that hangs on a hook next to the greeting cards. He paid with a ten and held his hand out for the change before I'd opened the till, the way people do when they need to get back to something.

I wouldn't have remembered it, except that the next Wednesday he bought a 5.

On nights you learn not to comment on what people buy. At three in the morning someone is always buying a pregnancy test, or Nurofen and a box of tissues, or laxatives and a Mars bar, and most of them would like the whole thing to feel about as personal as a vending machine. So I said nothing about the 5. I said nothing about the 6 either, although by then I had started thinking about it while I counted tablets.

My first guess was that he was counting something he'd given up. People in recovery mark their days and months, and I could picture a man lighting a candle at his kitchen table for each week without a drink. But he didn't look like someone keeping a private score. He looked tired in a more specific way. One week his jumper was on inside out. Another week he had a hospital visitor sticker on his chest, with the date written on it in biro, and he'd forgotten to peel it off.

The week he should have bought a 7, there weren't any.

I'd seen the hook earlier in the night and not thought about it. When he came in at ten past three, he went to the card aisle and stood there for longer than he needed to. Then he came to the counter with nothing in his hands.

"You don't have any sevens," he said.

"I can check out the back."

I knew there were none out the back. The party stock comes in a mixed carton every couple of months, and whoever orders it doesn't pay much attention to which numbers sell. I looked anyway, because he seemed to need me to, and came back with a packet of twenty-four plain candles with pink and white stripes.

"This is all we've got," I said. "Sorry."

He looked at the packet. "I only need one," he said. It didn't sound like a complaint. It sounded like he was working through a problem and this was one of the facts.

I opened the packet, took out one candle and gave it to him. He reached for his wallet and I told him not to worry about it. I'd write the packet off as damaged, which by then it was.

That was when he told me. I think he felt he owed me an explanation for a free candle, and I was curious enough to let him give one.

His son had been born at twenty-five weeks. He was in the neonatal unit across the road, in an incubator, and he'd weighed about as much as a block of butter when he arrived. The nurses there did something they called weekly birthdays. Every Wednesday, the day he was born, they wrote the number of weeks on a card and taped it to the side of the incubator. You can't have a naked flame in a neonatal unit, so he and his wife had started their own version in the car park. She had to express milk every three hours, day and night, and he drove her in for the three o'clock one. Afterwards they sat in the car on level three, lit the candle on the dashboard and sang to a photo on her phone.

"It's a bit stupid," he said.

I told him I didn't think it was stupid. People say that kind of thing to be polite, but I meant it.

After he left I wrote "number candles, check ALL digits" on the order sheet and underlined it twice. The day pharmacist texted me the next afternoon to ask if we were opening a party shop. She ordered them anyway. When the carton arrived I took out an 8 and a 9 and put them in the drawer under the register with the spare receipt rolls, in case someone's kid had a birthday on the weekend and the hook got cleared out again.

He came in the next Wednesday and I had the 8 on the counter before he reached the card aisle. He stopped and looked at it, then at me, and laughed through his nose. His son had come off the ventilator and was on CPAP now, which I knew enough about to know was good news. He paid for the candle and didn't wait for the change.

The week after that he bought the 9 and hardly spoke. He had the earlier look again. I didn't ask and he didn't offer, and I spent the rest of the night with a bad feeling I couldn't do anything with, so I reorganised the vitamins.

By the following Wednesday I had a 1 and a 0 in the drawer, held together with a rubber band. He didn't come in.

I tried not to make anything of it. There were plenty of ordinary reasons. His wife might have got a lift with someone else, or the feeds had moved to a different time, or he'd bought a full set at the supermarket in daylight like a sensible person. There were other reasons too, and on a night shift you have a lot of time to think about those. Around five I took the rubber band off the two candles, because leaving them bundled together started to feel presumptuous, and then I put it back on.

He came in the next night, Thursday, at about eleven. It was early for him and the wrong day. He wasn't wearing a visitor sticker. He put a digital thermometer and a packet of the smallest nappies we stock on the counter.

"They moved him," he said, before I could ask. "To special care. The step-down nursery." Parents couldn't stay overnight there, so his wife had been going in during the day. The doctors were talking about sending the baby home in two or three weeks, if he kept putting on weight and kept remembering to breathe while he fed, which apparently is something babies have to learn.

"So you missed the tenth," I said.

"Moving day," he said. "Nobody remembered until about nine last night, and by then we were both asleep on the couch."

I opened the drawer and put the 1 and the 0 on the counter next to the nappies. He picked them up, still in their rubber band, and turned them over.

"We'll do it tonight," he said. "Bit late."

He paid for everything, and this time he waited for his change. He put the candles in his shirt pocket and the rest in a bag, and said he'd probably see me next Wednesday, though he wasn't sure at what time anymore.

After he'd gone I went out the back and checked the carton. We had plenty of ones.
