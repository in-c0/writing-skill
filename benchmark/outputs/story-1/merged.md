# Number Candles

"Your guy's here," Marisol said, without looking up from the label printer. "Two fifty-one. He's late."

He wasn't late. He came in every Wednesday between ten to three and ten past, and this was nine minutes to. But Marisol had decided back in August that he belonged to us, and that we were owed an explanation, and she kept score.

I watched him in the round mirror over aisle nine. Same tan windbreaker, same slow walk past the greeting cards to the party shelf, where the Wilton number candles hang on little hooks for $1.99 each. He took one down, looked at it, put it back and took a different one. Then he came to the pharmacy counter, because after ten at night the front register closes and anything you buy in our store you buy from me.

He put a 7 on the counter.

"Seven," Marisol said under her breath, like she was calling bingo for herself.

"Is that everything?" I asked.

"That's it." He paid with two singles and waited for his penny. He always waited for the penny. "Have a good night, Doctor."

I'm not a doctor. I'm a PharmD, which my mother tells people is the same thing and which anybody with an actual MD will tell you is not. I said, "You too," and he went out through the automatic doors into the parking lot, where at three in the morning it was still ninety-one degrees.

Marisol peeled back the strip of receipt paper she kept taped under the counter and wrote the 7 at the end. The list read 4, 4, 9, 1, 0, 6, 6, 2, 5, 3, 3, 3, 8, 7.

"It's not a date," she said. "I tried dates. It's not a phone number unless he's calling Canada. I checked it against Powerball for the last three months."

"Maybe he likes candles."

"One candle a week. Nobody needs a zero, Joanne."

We knew his name. Two summers ago he'd filled lisinopril with us for a few months before his insurance switched him to mail order, and the system never lets anybody go. Ernesto Dalisay, born 1951. Marisol had reminded me of this more than once. She liked to point out that I could open his profile in about four seconds, and I liked to point out that I could lose my license in about the same amount of time, and that the district manager already left me a voicemail every Monday about our Sudafed counts and didn't need a second topic.

So we didn't look. We went back to work.

At three-thirty a man in a Cardinals jersey asked for Sudafed and didn't have ID, then had an ID that belonged to someone named Kimberly, then left. At ten past four a woman came in with a toddler asleep on her shoulder and a script from the urgent care on Seventh Street for amoxicillin. The little girl woke up when the mother shifted her to get her wallet and started crying, steady and heartbroken, the way they do when they have an ear infection and it is four in the morning and nobody will explain why. Marisol asked if she wanted it banana flavored and the mother said "God, yes," and Marisol flavored it. The overhead speakers played "Rich Girl" for the third time that shift. They play it about every forty minutes. I have looked into turning it off and I can't.

The truth is I had my own theory about Mr. Dalisay, and it was worse than Marisol's. I'd decided he was counting something sad. A child who died at a certain age, maybe, or the years since a wife was gone, and the numbers jumped around because grief does. I'd built a lot on that. I'm not proud of it. I hadn't told Marisol because she would have said it was dark, and she'd have been right, and then she'd have wanted to look him up even more.

The following Tuesday the Halloween reset came in. Two guys from a merchandising company spent the evening moving the party shelf to the back wall and filling the front of the store with plastic skulls and a seven-foot animated witch that cackled whenever anybody walked past it, including, at one point, me, four times in a row on my way to the bathroom. The number candles went into a tote in the stockroom. Nobody told me where.

Wednesday, two fifty-eight, he came in and stopped at the witch. She cackled at him. He looked at her for a while, then walked the store twice and came to the counter with nothing in his hands.

"The number candles," he said. "Are they gone?"

"They moved everything for Halloween," Marisol said, already off her stool. "Hang on."

She was in the back a long time. I could hear her knocking totes around. Mr. Dalisay stood at the counter with his hands folded on it, the way people stand at a hotel desk. The guard, Luis, who sits on a folding chair by the door and is mostly asleep after two, opened one eye to see what the noise was and closed it again.

Marisol came back with two candles: a 1 and a 0.

"This is all I've got in that tote," she said. "What number did you need?"

He looked at them. "A hundred," he said.

Marisol put both hands flat on the counter. "Sorry. A hundred?"

He seemed embarrassed, though not very. "My granddaughter. She's in Manila with her mom. When I get home it's suppertime there, and we do her birthday on the video call. She had a real one in August and she liked it, so now we do it every week. She tells me on the weekend how old she's going to be, and I get the candle, and she blows it out on her end. Through the phone."

"She's four?" I said, which was a guess.

"Four. Last week she was seven. Before that she was three for a while." He picked up the zero and turned it over like he was checking for a price. "On Sunday she told me she wanted to be a hundred. She gave me a lot of warning."

Marisol looked down at the receipt paper under the counter, and then up at me, and I could see her doing the whole list over again in her head. Four, four, nine, one. Zero.

"We have one zero," she said.

"I see that."

"Light the zero," Luis said from the door. We all turned around. He hadn't opened his eyes. "Then you move the phone over and light it again. She's four. She won't know it's the same one."

Mr. Dalisay considered this. "She might," he said. "She's sharp." But he slid both candles toward me, and I rang them up, three ninety-eight, and he paid with four singles and waited for his two pennies.

"Have a good night, Doctor," he said, and on the way out the witch cackled at him again, and he said something to her in Tagalog that made Luis laugh.

Marisol pulled the receipt paper off the underside of the counter and wrote 100 at the bottom, then drew a box around it.

"That doesn't count," she said. "That's two candles."

"It counts."

"Next week she's going to want to be a thousand."

I was still a little let down, which is not a nice thing to admit. I'd had a whole dead wife ready for him since September. I logged into the ordering system and found the Wilton candles, and I put in for six zeros, and then because I didn't know what a four-year-old would ask for next, I put in for one of everything else.
