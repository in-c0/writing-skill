# Market Day

The candles lived in a mason jar beside the register, the last remains of a spring when someone at regional decided the pharmacy should carry party supplies. The balloons sold first, then the streamers, then the paper plates printed with cartoon dinosaurs. The candles stayed. They were the thin spiral kind, pastel, sold loose at thirty-five cents apiece, and by the time Nadia took over the overnight shift the jar was the only evidence that a party aisle had ever existed.

She would not have noticed the man if he had bought anything else.

Nights at a twenty-four-hour pharmacy had a grammar, and she had learned it in her first month. Before midnight came the forgetful: insulin, birth control, the antibiotic someone had meant to collect at five. From midnight to two came the frightened, parents with hot-cheeked toddlers on their hips who asked her to read the dosing chart aloud, as if the numbers might be kinder in her voice. After two came the lonely, who bought gum and lingered by the reading glasses.

And at ten past three every Wednesday morning came a tall man in a gray work jacket who walked straight to the register, studied the jar for a moment as though the color mattered, chose one candle, and paid with two quarters. He never waited for his change. He never wanted a bag.

For the first few weeks she thought nothing of it. By the second month she had theories, which is what happens to anyone who spends eight hours a night under fluorescent light with only the hum of the coolers for company. A widower, marking something weekly that used to be yearly. A child in a hospital somewhere, each week another week survived. A man in recovery, building his own private stack of chips. She liked the recovery theory best. It made the jar feel useful.

She learned his name in October, when he came in at the usual time with a prescription from the urgent care down the road. Amoxicillin. "A tooth," he said, touching his jaw, a little embarrassed, as though pain were a kind of bad manners. She typed him into the system.

DENG, SIMON. DOB 01/01/1987.

Nadia had been a pharmacist for eleven years, and she knew about January firsts. The software flagged them sometimes as probable errors, but they usually weren't. January first was the date written down when nobody knew the real one. She had seen it on an Afghan family of six, on a Somali grandmother who laughed when Nadia wished her happy new year, on four brothers once, all born on January first, years apart. Her preceptor in pharmacy school had told her, the first time she'd reached to correct one, *Leave it. That's not a typo. That's a history.*

She filled the amoxicillin. He took a candle too. Pink, that week.

---

She didn't ask for another month. Asking felt like reaching into someone's coat pocket. But one Wednesday in November it was raining hard enough that he came in shaking water from his sleeves, and the store was empty, and the coolers were humming, and the question came out of her before she had decided to let it.

"Can I ask what they're for?"

He considered the question the way he considered the jar.

"Birthday," he said.

"Whose?"

"Mine." He smiled at whatever her face did. "Maybe."

He told it plainly, the way people tell things they have told many times and have finally sanded smooth. He had been five, or six, when he walked out of South Sudan with a column of other boys. Ethiopia first, then Kenya, then a camp called Kakuma, where he spent most of what should have been his childhood. When the resettlement officers came, years later, they needed birthdays for the paperwork, and none of the boys had one they could prove.

"So they gave us all January first," he said. "Thousands of us. Same day. I go to the DMV, the woman says, *Happy birthday soon.* At my church in Omaha, every man in the back row, same birthday. We used to joke we should rent one hall."

"But you don't celebrate January first."

"January first belongs to the paperwork." He picked a candle out of the jar, yellow, and turned it in his fingers. "My mother said one thing about the day I was born. I remember because I was small and I was listening when I should have been sleeping. She told my aunt I came on market day. *He came on market day, so he will always be hungry.*" He laughed, a short, warm sound. "She was right."

"And market day was—"

"Wednesday. In our town, Wednesday." He set two quarters on the counter. "So it was a Wednesday. One of them."

Nadia did the math without meaning to. "Fifty-two chances a year."

"Fifty-three, some years." He seemed pleased that she'd thought of it. "Those are good years."

He worked nights too, he said, loading trucks at the distribution center out by the interstate. He lit the candle in the cab of his pickup before his shift, let it burn down on the dashboard while he ate, then went in. No cake. No song.

"And a wish?"

He shook his head. "I'm forty. Or thirty-nine. At my age a wish is greedy. I just say thank you."

"To who?"

He shrugged, already turning toward the door, toward the rain. "To whichever Wednesday it is."

---

In December a memo came down from regional about discontinued inventory. The candles were on the list, with a note to clear them by end of quarter. Nadia read the memo twice, then counted the jar. Forty-one candles. Ten months, give or take, if nobody else bought any, and nobody else bought any.

On her day off she drove to the party supply store in the strip mall by the freeway and bought three boxes of the spiral kind, pastel, twenty-four to a box. She paid for them herself. The next night she poured them into the jar, and if the assistant manager noticed the jar was fuller than it had been in a year, he didn't mention it. Some things you can only get away with at night.

The following Wednesday, Mr. Deng stopped in front of the register and looked at the jar for a long moment. Then he looked at her. He didn't say anything about it, and she was grateful, because she wouldn't have known what to say back.

He chose a blue one. Two quarters on the counter.

"Happy birthday, Mr. Deng," she said.

"Maybe," he said.

"Maybe," she agreed.

She watched him through the front window as he crossed the lot. In the cab of his truck a match flared, then settled into one small, steady flame on the dashboard, and she stood at the register with the coolers humming behind her until it went out.
