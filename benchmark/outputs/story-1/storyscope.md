# Nineteen Cents

"Just this," he said, and put the candle on the counter like it might roll away.

That was the first Tuesday, or the first one I noticed. It was 2:40 in the morning and the front registers were chained off, so anything anybody bought after midnight came through me, which corporate calls "pharmacy-led overnight retail" and I call being a cashier with a doctorate.

The candle came from the bin. Kevin, the day manager, had put a plastic tub of loose birthday candles next to the pharmacy register in August, left over from a reset when the party aisle shrank to make room for phone chargers. Striped, pastel, nineteen cents each. Nobody bought them. Kids took them and chewed on them while their parents waited on amoxicillin.

He paid with a dollar, took the change and the receipt, folded the receipt in half and left. Older guy, Carhartt jacket gone pale at the cuffs. He didn't take a bag. You can't really bag a birthday candle.

The next Tuesday he came in at 2:35. "Just this."

By the fourth week Marisol had a theory. Marisol was my tech on Tuesdays until one, and on nights she was behind on her prereqs she stayed till two and did flashcards in the consult booth.

"Dead wife," she said. "Her birthday was a Tuesday. He lights one every week because once a year isn't enough."

"That's insane."

"It's romantic."

"It's insane. Nobody grieves on a weekly schedule. You'd burn out."

"You'd know," she said, which I let go, because she was twenty-three and had once watched me eat a whole sleeve of Fig Newtons over the sink in the back.

My theory was sobriety. People in the program count days, then weeks. A candle a week, a little private cake with nothing under it. I liked my theory better than hers because it had a structure, and because it meant he was winning at something.

Dale, who did overnight security from a folding chair by the automatic doors and was technically employed by a different company, said, "He's cheap."

"Cheap how? It's one candle."

"So that's cheap," Dale said, and went back to his phone, where a lot of men were yelling about the Jets.

We each put five dollars in an empty Mucinex box and wrote our names on the flaps.

He wasn't my only regular. There was the nurse from Yale New Haven who bought a Red Bull and a Snickers at 3:15 and called me "chief." There was a man who came in every few nights to ask whether we had any Sudafed behind the counter, knowing full well what I'd say, as if the answer might change with the shift. There was the lady with the Pomeranian in a tote bag, which is not allowed, which nobody has ever told her. None of them got a Mucinex box. They made sense. The candle didn't, and on a night shift something that doesn't make sense is worth more than coffee.

I'll admit I got invested. I started topping up the candle bin on Monday nights. Kevin had a backup case in the stockroom, a hundred and forty-four candles, which at the current rate would see the man through to 2029. I moved the pinks to the front, because the first three weeks he'd taken a pink and I'd decided that meant something. Week six he took a blue. I spent most of a slow hour deciding what that meant too.

I also got slightly worse at my actual job between 2:30 and 2:45. A girl in a UConn hoodie came in for Plan B that week and I made her wait while I watched the door, and then I felt bad and told her it worked better the sooner she took it, which she knew, and which she told me she knew, in a tone I deserved.

He never said anything but "Just this" and "Thanks." Cash, always a single bill, and he waited for the receipt even when I tried to hand him the change first.

I'm a pharmacist. I read the fine print for a living. You'd think.

Week eleven, at 2:20, a woman came in with a baby in a car seat and a thermometer still in her hand, asking how much infant Tylenol. The baby was seven weeks old, which meant the answer wasn't Tylenol, it was the ER on Whitney Avenue, and I spent ten minutes being firm and gentle in the right proportions while she cried and asked if I was sure. I was sure. Dale walked her to her car and came back and sat down without saying anything, and that's the most I've ever liked Dale.

So I wasn't watching at 2:35. I looked up and he was already at the counter, a yellow one this time, and I rang it up, and I was too tired to be careful, and I said, "Can I ask what they're for?"

He looked at me for a second like he was picking which answer to give. Then he turned the receipt over and put his finger on the bottom.

TELL US HOW WE DID! Complete our survey within 7 days for a chance to win $5,000. One entry per household per week.

"Need a receipt," he said. "This is the cheapest thing in the store. Gum's a dollar twenty-nine."

"You come in at two-thirty in the morning to do a customer satisfaction survey."

"I drive for the bakery on Fenn Street. You're on the way." He tucked the receipt into his jacket. "You get four stars, mostly. One time you got two. Night you didn't say hi."

I didn't know what to do with that, so I said, "Have you ever won?"

"Not yet."

"What would you do with five thousand dollars?"

He thought about it longer than I expected. "Pay my sister back," he said. Then: "Not all of it." He held up the yellow candle. "My granddaughter keeps these. She's got a mason jar about half full. She thinks I go to a lot of parties."

He crossed the lot to a white box truck with the bakery's name stenciled on the side, minus the S, and pulled out onto Route 9 without signaling.

Marisol had gone home at one. I texted her *survey*. She sent back a row of question marks, then, a minute later, *NO*.

Dale was already holding out his hand. I gave him the Mucinex box. He counted it, which was unnecessary, and put the ten dollars in his wallet. He didn't gloat, which was worse.

"How'd you know?"

"Didn't," Dale said. "Just figured it wasn't a story."

At 4:10 I dug an old receipt out of my wallet, a Diet Coke from out front, and did the survey on my phone. Employees aren't eligible. I gave the overnight pharmacist five stars. In the comments box I wrote that she pays attention to her regulars, then deleted it and typed *n/a*.
